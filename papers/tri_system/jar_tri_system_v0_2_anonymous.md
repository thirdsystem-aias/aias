# Abstract

Brand science has rested for three decades on two systems of brand presence — Mental Availability and Physical Availability — that describe brand position on consumer-mediated retrieval surfaces. AI-mediated retrieval introduces a different surface: when a consumer acts on an LLM-based recommendation, the retrieval is performed by the intermediary system rather than by consumer memory or against the consumer's purchase environment. This paper proposes a tri-system extension of the Ehrenberg-Bass framework, preserving Mental and Physical Availability unchanged and adding AI Availability as a third construct governed by two hypotheses — H1 Conditional Reweighting and H2 Default Reinforcement — and moderated by Identity Load. Each claim carries explicit falsification conditions. The construct-validity warrant rests on pre-registered evidence from the AIAS Presence Measurement Programme: the Regime 4 signature (|bivariate Spearman ρ| < 0.35; partial ρ < 0 under covariate control) is confirmed in three independent category studies and deepens under panel-expansion robustness. The evidence supports AI Availability as a distinct empirical object; it does not test H1 or H2 directly, each of which sets a specific empirical agenda.

**Keywords:** AI Availability; Mental Availability; Physical Availability; tri-system framework; brand growth; Conditional Reweighting; Default Reinforcement; Identity Load; Ehrenberg-Bass; pre-registration

**JEL classification:** M31 (primary); L86; L15; D83; M37

# Management Slant

- **Brand-tracking instrumentation built on Mental Availability and Physical Availability alone now misses a measurable, share-determining surface in any category where AI assistants mediate purchase decisions.** Audit which of your brand's categories are AI-mediated and at what penetration; the higher the penetration, the larger the share variance your current tracker cannot read.

- **Different categories migrate to AI-mediated selection at different rates, and the rate is predictable.** Low-Identity-Load categories (commodities, technical purchases, low-stake repeats) migrate fastest; high-Identity-Load categories (fragrance, single-malt whisky, engagement rings) resist longest. Forecast portfolio exposure category by category, not as a single brand-wide question.

- **First-mover advantage in AI retrieval tends to compound.** Once a brand achieves dominance in AI Availability rankings, three reinforcing mechanisms — training-data feedback, grounding-source weighting, and behavioural lock-in — make the position sticky. The window for late-mover catch-up narrows over time.

- **The Binet-Field long-short allocation framework generalises from two capitals to three.** Investment in AI Availability — through training-data representation, grounding-source coverage, structural-prior alignment, and category-density management — sits alongside brand-building and activation as a distinct capital category with its own optimal allocation conditional on category position.

- **AI Availability is measurable now.** The AIAS Presence Measurement Protocol provides a pre-registered methodology for tracking brand position inside AI retrieval; the Regime 4 construct-validity evidence across three categories supports treating AI Availability as an empirically distinct object suitable for inclusion in brand dashboards.

# 1. Introduction

Brand-tracking practice in 2026 reports an empirical pattern that the prevailing two-system model of brand growth is not equipped to interpret. Mental Availability tracker scores rise across consecutive waves; category share is flat or declining; Physical Availability is unchanged on every distribution metric the tracker records. The brand has done what the framework prescribes. The framework predicts share movement. The market does not produce it.

The pattern is reported with increasing frequency in categories where a third party — an AI assistant — sits between the consumer's stated preference and the brand selected. When a buyer asks an LLM-based assistant which brand to consider in a category and acts on the response, the brand chosen is not retrieved from memory (Mental Availability) and not encountered in a retail environment (Physical Availability); it is produced by an intermediary system whose retrieval mechanics are not described by either construct.

This paper proposes that AI-mediated retrieval has introduced a third axis of brand presence — AI Availability — and that the Ehrenberg-Bass two-system model (Sharp 2010; Sharp and Romaniuk 2021) is best extended rather than replaced to accommodate it. The argument is conservative in stance: Mental and Physical Availability are imported unchanged, with their operational definitions and the empirical generalisations that rest on them. The argument is ambitious in claim: a third system, operating by its own dynamics, is necessary to describe brand growth in categories where AI mediation has become material.

Two hypotheses govern the system's behaviour. **H1 — Conditional Reweighting** proposes that the weight of each system in driving purchase choice is conditional on AI-mediation penetration in the category and on the category's Identity Load. **H2 — Default Reinforcement** proposes that once a brand achieves a position of dominance in AI Availability, the position compounds through three reinforcing mechanisms: training-data feedback, grounding-source weighting, and behavioural lock-in. Identity Load — the degree to which the selection process is itself part of what the purchase accomplishes — moderates the rate of reweighting across categories. Each claim carries explicit falsification conditions, specified in Section 3 and consolidated in Section 6.

The mechanism-evidence basis for the proposed extension rests on the AIAS Presence Measurement Programme, a pre-registered, versioned construct-validity programme operationalising the Presence component of AI Availability against an external consumer-search reference (Google Trends). The programme has documented a regime — covariate-saturated weak — in which AI retrieval and consumer-search interest are nearly orthogonal at the brand level and reveal a small negative residual under brand-age and tier controls. The regime has been confirmed in three pre-registered category studies — premium facial skincare, personal-finance applications, and premium tea — and survives panel-expansion robustness on the most recent (premium tea, 28-brand registry, six tradition cells). The construct-validity interpretation is that AI retrieval is not reducible to existing consumer-salience constructs, which establishes the warrant for the tri-system extension. This is mechanism evidence for the framework's distinctness from existing constructs, not proof of either H1 or H2.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical background — the Ehrenberg-Bass empirical generalisations the paper extends, and the cognitive-offloading and automation-bias literatures that ground AI mediation as a substrate change rather than a channel addition. Section 3 specifies the tri-system framework: the formal definition of AI Availability and its four inputs, H1 and H2 with their falsification conditions, and Identity Load as moderator. Section 4 reports the mechanism evidence from the AIAS programme. Section 5 discusses implications and boundary conditions for the empirical generalisations the paper extends. Section 6 consolidates falsification conditions and evidentiary class distinctions. Section 7 concludes.

# 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1 The two-system model and its empirical generalisations

The Ehrenberg-Bass research programme has, over three decades, established that brand growth is driven primarily by penetration rather than loyalty, that buying patterns within a category follow a small number of regularities (the double-jeopardy law, the duplication-of-purchase law, the natural-monopoly law), and that brand-share differences are reducible largely to differences in how easily a brand is brought to mind in a buying situation and how easily it is found and selected at the point of purchase (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt, and Barwise 1990; Sharp 2010; Romaniuk and Sharp 2016; Sharp and Romaniuk 2021). The two constructs that carry the framework operationally are Mental Availability — the probability that the brand is retrieved in category-relevant decision contexts, measured through category-entry-point salience instruments (Romaniuk 2003; Romaniuk 2018) — and Physical Availability — the probability that the brand is available to be purchased once chosen, measured through distribution coverage and findability. Their joint product approximates category share; brand-tracking practice has built its instrumentation around the two measurement programmes for thirty years.

Binet and Field (2013, 2017) extend the operational framework into media-allocation decisions: long-term brand-building (the construction of Mental Availability) and short-term activation (conversion against Physical Availability) are the two capital categories that drive brand growth, and an optimal allocation ratio has been characterised empirically across hundreds of cases. The empirical generalisations underwriting the two-system model are robust, replicated, and consequential. This paper preserves them.

What the two-system model does not specify is the retrieval substrate. Both constructs assume that the consumer is the retriever — that brand decisions emerge from consumer cognition (memory) operating on a consumer-faced choice environment (shelf, page, app). The substrate is implicit because, until recently, it was uncontested.

## 2.2 AI mediation as substrate change

AI-mediated retrieval introduces a different retrieval substrate. When a buyer poses a category-recommendation query to an LLM-based assistant and acts on the response, the retrieval is not performed by the consumer's memory and not exercised against the consumer's purchase environment; it is performed by the intermediary system, against the system's training-data representation and grounding sources, and presented to the consumer as a small set of named options. This is not a new channel additional to existing channels — it is a structural change in who or what does the retrieving.

The change is consistent with two established literatures that the brand-science programme has not yet integrated. The cognitive-offloading literature documents that humans systematically delegate memory and search tasks to external systems when those systems are available and judged reliable (Clark and Chalmers 1998; Sparrow, Liu, and Wegner 2011; Risko and Gilbert 2016). Offloading reduces the cognitive cost of decision-making and tends to be sticky: once a search task has been delegated, it is re-delegated rather than re-internalised. The automation-bias literature documents that humans systematically over-rely on automated recommendations — particularly under time pressure, uncertainty, or cognitive load — and that the over-reliance is not corrected by knowledge that the system is imperfect (Mosier and Skitka 1996; Parasuraman and Manzey 2010; Goddard, Roudsari, and Wyatt 2012). Both mechanisms operate on the cognitive economy that Mental Availability instrumentation is designed to measure. The presence of a usable, sufficiently-trusted AI assistant changes the rate at which Mental Availability is exercised in the buying situation.

For the brand, the implication is structural. Mental Availability instrumentation measures the brand's position in consumer memory; it does not measure the brand's position in the assistant's training-data representation, grounding sources, or output distribution. Physical Availability instrumentation measures the brand's distribution coverage in retail; it does not measure the brand's recommendation rate inside AI responses. Neither construct's operational definition admits the new retrieval surface, and neither construct's measurement instrument observes it.

This is the gap the paper addresses. A third construct — AI Availability — is proposed in Section 3 to describe the brand's position inside the AI retrieval substrate, with the explicit stipulation that Mental Availability and Physical Availability remain unchanged in their definitions and operational measurements. The motivation for a third construct is not that the existing two are wrong; it is that the existing two were not built to describe the substrate that AI mediation has introduced.

# 3. The Tri-System Framework

## 3.1 AI Availability: construct and inputs

AI Availability is defined as the brand-level probability that a brand is retrieved, recommended, or selected by an AI intermediary in a category-relevant decision context. The construct is analogous in form to Mental Availability and Physical Availability — a probability over a defined decision context — and analogous in scope: it is a category-level brand property, not a campaign-level metric. It is operationally distinct from both. Mental Availability is the probability over the consumer's memory; Physical Availability is the probability over the consumer's purchase environment; AI Availability is the probability over the intermediary system's output distribution.

The construct is governed by four inputs, each of which is a brand-level property that can in principle be measured independently.

*Representation in training data.* The brand's presence and characterisation in the corpora on which the LLM was trained — including web content, product reviews, editorial coverage, technical documentation, and reference materials. Training-data representation establishes the prior probability that the model surfaces the brand absent grounding.

*Coverage in grounding sources.* The brand's presence in retrieval-augmented sources reachable by the system at inference — web search indexes, knowledge graphs, vertical databases. Grounding coverage modulates the prior at the point of response and is the input most directly susceptible to brand-side action.

*Structural priors of the retrieval system.* System-level mechanics that affect the surfacing of any brand — recency weighting, ranking heuristics, output-length constraints, citation policies, safety filters. Structural priors are exogenous to the brand but shape what is retrievable for any brand in the category.

*Competitive density.* The number of brands the system can plausibly surface within a category and decision context, given output-length and answer-format constraints. Competitive density is the categorical analogue of shelf-space: when the assistant returns three brands, the four-input determination of which three runs against the carrying capacity of the response.

The four inputs together produce a brand's AI Availability. They are causes of position. They are not the same as the six observable dimensions of position — Presence, Ranking, Consistency, Coverage, Grounding, and Sentiment — that together compose the AIAS measurement instrument ([Author] 2026b). The four inputs explain why a brand sits where it does in AI retrieval; the six components describe where it sits.

## 3.2 H1 — Conditional Reweighting

**H1.** *The weight of each system in driving purchase choice is conditional on (a) AI-mediation penetration in the category and (b) the category's Identity Load. As AI-mediation penetration rises and Identity Load is low, the weight of AI Availability in the choice function rises and the weights of Mental and Physical Availability decline correspondingly.*

The hypothesis preserves the existing two-system model as the limiting case. In categories where AI mediation is absent, AI Availability carries zero weight and the two-system model holds unchanged. In categories where AI mediation has saturated and the selection process is separable from the product, AI Availability becomes the dominant determinant of the brand's share-conditional probability of selection. The two-system model is not wrong in those categories; it is partial. Mental Availability and Physical Availability continue to operate, but their weights in the choice function are smaller because the retrieval substrate has shifted.

The hypothesis is testable category by category, in principle through three measurements: AI-mediation penetration (the share of category-relevant decisions involving AI mediation), Identity Load (operationalisation discussed in §3.4), and the relative contribution of each system to explained variance in brand share. The category-level prediction is that the partial elasticity of share with respect to AI Availability rises monotonically in AI penetration and falls in Identity Load.

**Falsification.** H1 is falsified for a category if, with AI penetration measured at a non-trivial level and Identity Load operationalised, AI Availability contributes no incremental explanatory variance to brand share beyond Mental and Physical Availability. H1 is falsified as a general claim if, across a stratified sample of categories spanning the AI-penetration × Identity-Load space, the predicted monotonic structure of system weights is not observed.

H1 is not yet directly tested. The AIAS programme supplies construct-validity evidence for AI Availability as a distinct measurable construct (§4); it does not yet supply the share-explained-variance decomposition that H1 specifies. The decomposition is the empirical agenda H1 sets.

## 3.3 H2 — Default Reinforcement

**H2.** *Once a brand achieves a position of dominance in AI Availability — operationally, a position in the top-N response slots that is stable across measurement waves — the position tends to compound through three reinforcing mechanisms: training-data feedback, grounding-source weighting, and behavioural lock-in.*

*Training-data feedback.* Brands surfaced by AI assistants attract documentary attention — coverage, reviews, references, recommendations — at higher rates than non-surfaced brands of comparable size. Documentary attention enters the next training cycle of the underlying models, raising the trained-prior probability of the brand being surfaced in subsequent versions. The feedback runs from output to input on a delay matched to the corpus-revision cadence of frontier models.

*Grounding-source weighting.* Retrieval-augmented systems weight grounding sources by source authority and brand–source association. Dominant brands accumulate authoritative source coverage at higher rates than non-dominant brands and are therefore over-represented in the grounding-source mix the system consults at inference. The bias compounds: the more dominant the brand, the more weight its sources carry; the more weight its sources carry, the more dominant the brand becomes in output.

*Behavioural lock-in.* Consumers who have acted on AI recommendations and been satisfied repeat the recommendation request and accept subsequent recommendations at higher rates. Both effects narrow the band within which alternative brands compete to be surfaced for the same consumer in subsequent queries — a recommendation-slot persistence effect that complements the corpus-level compounding above.

The three mechanisms operate on different timescales and through different infrastructure. Training-data feedback compounds at the cadence of model retraining. Grounding-source weighting compounds at the cadence of retrieval-system updates and source-database refreshes. Behavioural lock-in compounds at the cadence of consumer querying. Their joint effect is a tendency, not a determinism — and a tendency that operates against any countervailing force the category supplies (regulatory intervention, category disruption, model substitution).

**Falsification.** H2 predicts rank stability over time among brands at the top of AI Availability rankings, contingent on no exogenous shock. The hypothesis is falsified for a category if AI Availability rankings show high inter-wave rank turnover — operationally, Spearman rank correlation below 0.7 across measurement waves separated by a measurement-cycle interval, in the absence of identifiable exogenous shocks. The hypothesis is falsified as a general claim if, across categories with measured AI penetration above a non-trivial threshold, rank stability is not systematically higher than would be predicted by a null model with no reinforcement.

The AIAS programme has documented strong rank stability across panel expansion in premium tea (Spearman ρ = 0.938 / 0.949 between v0.14 and v0.15 measurement waves on the overlap brands; [Author] 2026d) — a finding consistent with H2 but obtained under conditions of registry expansion rather than temporal separation. The temporal-stability test specified by H2 is forthcoming programme work.

## 3.4 Identity Load as moderator

Identity Load is defined as the degree to which the selection process — the search, evaluation, and decision activity preceding purchase — is itself part of what the purchase accomplishes for the buyer. In high-Identity-Load categories, the process is constitutive of the purchase: a fragrance is partly chosen for the experience of choosing it; an engagement ring is in part the work of arriving at the choice; a single-malt whisky carries the ritual of selection. In low-Identity-Load categories, the process is instrumental: a USB-C charger needs to work; a surge protector needs the right number of outlets; a paper-towel brand needs to be in stock.

The moderator hypothesis is that Conditional Reweighting (H1) proceeds at a rate inversely proportional to Identity Load. In low-Identity-Load categories, consumers offload the selection process to AI assistants readily because the process is friction; AI penetration rises quickly; the weight of AI Availability in the choice function rises quickly. In high-Identity-Load categories, consumers resist offloading because the process is the point; AI penetration rises more slowly; the weight of AI Availability rises more slowly even where the technical capability for AI mediation exists.

Identity Load is the framework component most in need of operational specification. A preliminary operationalisation has three components: (1) consumer survey measures of the extent to which the purchase process is described as ritualistic, identity-relevant, or "the point of the purchase"; (2) ethnographic observation of decision episodes within the category; (3) choice-experiment designs in which AI-mediation availability is varied and the consumer's elected use of mediation is observed. The three measurement modes are independent, and convergent validity across them is the operational standard.

**Falsification.** Identity Load is falsified as a useful moderator if, across categories spanning the Identity-Load measurement range and at comparable levels of AI-mediation availability, the rate of Conditional Reweighting does not vary systematically with the measured Identity Load score. The moderator is also falsified if the three measurement modes do not converge: a high-Identity-Load reading on survey measures should correspond to ethnographic observation of identity-relevant selection behaviour and to low elected mediation use in choice experiments.

# 4. Mechanism Evidence: AI Availability as a Distinct Construct

The framework specified in Section 3 makes a positive claim about a measurable construct: that AI Availability exists as a brand-level property distinct from Mental and Physical Availability. The claim is not testable without an operational measurement. The AIAS Presence Measurement Programme ([Author] 2026a, 2026b) supplies the Presence component of that measurement — the rate at which a brand appears across matched LLM responses to category-recommendation prompts — and pre-registers per-category construct-validity tests against an external consumer-search reference. This section reports the programme's central finding and specifies precisely what it supports.

## 4.1 The construct-validity question

The AIAS Presence Measurement Protocol v1.2 ([Author] 2026b) specifies a category-level construct-validity test. Its central object is the bivariate Spearman rank correlation between AI Presence and Google Trends consumer-search interest, computed at two measurement waves separated by seven days, with a partial Spearman correlation adding brand-age and brand-tier (or tradition) covariate controls. The question is whether AI Presence tracks an established external measure of consumer-level brand salience. If AI Availability were a re-coding of existing consumer-salience signals, the bivariate correlation would be high across categories and would survive controls. If AI Availability were a distinct construct produced by mechanisms specific to AI retrieval, the correlation structure would vary across categories in ways the existing consumer-salience constructs cannot predict.

## 4.2 The Regime 4 signature

The programme has documented four empirical regimes structuring the AI Presence–Trends relationship across categories. One regime — *covariate-saturated weak*, designated Regime 4 — exhibits a specific signature: |bivariate Spearman ρ| < 0.35 at both waves, and partial Spearman ρ < 0 after covariate control. AI Presence and consumer-search interest are nearly orthogonal at the brand level; under covariate control the relationship reveals a small negative residual. AI surfaces a different brand mix than English-language consumer search captures, and the difference is not explained by age or tier confounds.

The signature has been confirmed in three pre-registered category studies. Premium facial skincare ([Author] 2026c) returned bivariate ρ = 0.282 / 0.332 across two waves with partial ρ = −0.205 / −0.117 — positive bivariate flipping negative under controls. Personal-finance applications ([Author] 2026c) returned bivariate ρ = 0.168 / 0.094 and partial ρ = −0.159 / −0.287. Premium tea ([Author] 2026e) returned bivariate ρ = −0.066 / −0.134 and partial ρ = −0.084 / −0.146 — the cleanest case, with negative correlation prior to any covariate adjustment. The three categories span structurally different markets (beauty retail, financial services, specialty food and beverage); the regime reproduces across them with the same signature.

## 4.3 Panel-expansion robustness

A separate threat to validity is panel composition: that the Regime 4 result depends on which brands the registry includes. The most recent programme study ([Author] 2026d) addresses this directly, replicating the premium-tea result on a registry-expanded 28-brand panel across six tradition cells. The result deepens: bivariate ρ = −0.150 / −0.200; partial ρ = −0.236 / −0.273. Panel expansion intensified the inverse co-movement rather than weakening it. Brand-level AI Presence rankings on the overlap with the prior wave remain stable at Spearman ρ = 0.938 / 0.949 — evidence that the regime classification is not an artefact of brand selection within the category.

## 4.4 What the evidence supports

Across three independent pre-registered category studies plus a panel-expansion robustness test, AI Presence does not reduce to consumer-search interest at the brand level. The relationship is sometimes weakly positive, sometimes weakly negative, never uniformly high in the way a re-coding interpretation would predict. The partial-correlation residual under age and tier controls is small and negative across all four results — consistent with AI retrieval surfacing a brand mix that existing consumer-salience covariates do not predict.

The interpretation supports the warrant for a third construct: AI Availability is not reducible to existing consumer-salience constructs and is a distinct empirical object measurable in its own right. The interpretation does not support H1 (Conditional Reweighting) or H2 (Default Reinforcement) directly. H1 requires a share-explained-variance decomposition that construct-validity evidence does not supply. H2 requires temporal-stability measurement across measurement-cycle intervals; the panel-expansion stability finding is consistent with H2 but is not its direct test. The Regime 4 evidence is mechanism support for the framework's distinctness from existing constructs, not proof of either hypothesis. The empirical agenda H1 and H2 set is forthcoming programme work.

# 5. Discussion and Implications

The tri-system extension has implications for the empirical generalisations the Ehrenberg-Bass programme has established, for the budget-allocation logic that has carried operational planning for a decade, and for the category-level research agenda the framework now sets.

## 5.1 The Ehrenberg-Bass empirical generalisations under AI mediation

The empirical generalisations underwriting the two-system model — double jeopardy, duplication of purchase, natural monopoly, and the penetration-versus-loyalty pattern — were derived from consumer-side panel and survey data over decades (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt, and Barwise 1990; Sharp 2010; Romaniuk and Sharp 2016). They describe regularities in how consumers behave across brands within a category. They do not describe regularities in how intermediary systems behave across brands within a category. The two surfaces are different.

The framework's claim is not that the Ehrenberg-Bass generalisations are wrong for the brand-share component that remains consumer-mediated. The claim is that the share-determining surface itself has expanded. In a category at zero AI penetration, the empirical generalisations operate on the entirety of the relevant retrieval surface. In a category at non-trivial AI penetration, a portion of share-determining retrieval moves to a surface whose dynamics are not yet characterised by the empirical generalisations and may not be characterised by them in the same way.

The natural-monopoly law illustrates the question. In consumer-mediated retrieval, large brands enjoy disproportionate share of light buyers — a finding so robust it is treated as a generalisation. In AI-mediated retrieval, the analogous question is whether large brands enjoy disproportionate share of AI recommendations to light buyers. H2 (Default Reinforcement) predicts the AI-mediated answer is yes; the rank-stability evidence in §4.3 is consistent with that prediction; but transitivity from the consumer-side generalisation to the AI-mediated surface requires direct measurement. The paper's position is that each of the Ehrenberg-Bass generalisations should be tested separately for its AI-mediated analogue rather than assumed to transfer.

## 5.2 Identity Load and the rate of transition

The framework predicts that the rate of Conditional Reweighting varies inversely with Identity Load. The substantive implication is that different categories cross into the regime where AI Availability is share-material at different rates, and the cross-category variation is not random.

Three empirically observable strata follow. Low-Identity-Load categories — replaceable commodities, technical purchases, low-stake repeats — should migrate fastest: the selection process is friction, consumers offload readily, and AI penetration rises in advance of category-side adaptation. Mid-Identity-Load categories — discretionary purchases with some ritual content but no constitutive process — migrate at intermediate rates and produce the most analytically interesting transitions. High-Identity-Load categories — fragrance, single-malt whisky, engagement rings, certain wine categories — should resist mediation longest; the selection process is constitutive of the purchase and is not separable from the product.

The AIAS programme's category selection to date — premium tea, premium facial skincare, personal-finance applications, project management software, premium running shoes, premium olive oil — has clustered in the low-to-mid Identity-Load range. The construct-validity findings reported in §4 are therefore strongest for the strata where AI mediation is most expected to be material. The framework's predictions at the high-Identity-Load end are forthcoming empirical work.

## 5.3 Budget allocation and the long-short framework

Binet and Field (2013, 2017) operationalised brand-growth investment as a two-capital problem: long-term brand-building (Mental Availability) and short-term activation (Physical Availability). The empirical generalisation has carried marketing-planning practice for a decade. If AI Availability is a third capital category, the allocation logic generalises. Investment in AI Availability — operationally, in the four inputs specified in §3.1 — sits adjacent to brand-building and activation, distinct from both, with its own optimal allocation conditional on category position in the AI-penetration × Identity-Load space. The derivation of the allocation rule depends on share-decomposition evidence the AIAS programme has not yet supplied; the structural point is that the two-capital allocation generalises to three as categories migrate.

## 5.4 The research agenda

The framework sets a specific empirical agenda. (1) The share-explained-variance decomposition that H1 requires — measurement of the relative contribution of Mental, Physical, and AI Availability to brand share, category by category, at varied levels of AI penetration. (2) The temporal-stability measurement that H2 requires — Spearman rank correlation of AI Availability rankings across measurement-cycle intervals, in categories at non-trivial AI penetration. (3) Operationalisation of Identity Load through the three measurement modes specified in §3.4, and convergent-validity testing across them. (4) Extension of the AIAS construct-validity programme into high-Identity-Load categories, where the framework's predictions are sharpest and least tested.

Each item is a separate study, in some cases a separate research programme. The framework is a starting position for the agenda, not a closure of it.

# 6. Falsification and Evidentiary Classes

## 6.1 Falsification conditions, consolidated

**H1 (Conditional Reweighting).** Falsified for a category if AI Availability contributes no incremental explanatory variance to brand share beyond Mental and Physical Availability, with AI penetration measured at a non-trivial level and Identity Load operationalised. Falsified as a general claim if the predicted monotonic structure of system weights in AI-penetration × Identity-Load space is not observed across a stratified sample of categories.

**H2 (Default Reinforcement).** Falsified for a category if AI Availability rank stability across measurement-cycle intervals falls below Spearman ρ = 0.7 in the absence of identifiable exogenous shocks. Falsified as a general claim if rank stability across categories with non-trivial AI penetration is not systematically higher than a null model with no reinforcement would predict.

**Identity Load (moderator).** Falsified if the rate of Conditional Reweighting does not vary systematically with the measured Identity Load score across categories at comparable AI-mediation availability. Falsified separately if survey, ethnographic, and choice-experiment operationalisations of Identity Load do not show convergent validity.

| **H1** — Conditional Reweighting | AI Availability contributes no incremental explanatory variance to brand share beyond Mental and Physical Availability, with AI penetration at a non-trivial level and Identity Load operationalised | Across a stratified sample of categories spanning the AI-penetration × Identity-Load space, the predicted monotonic structure of system weights is not observed |
| **H2** — Default Reinforcement | AI Availability rank stability across measurement-cycle intervals falls below Spearman ρ = 0.7 in the absence of identifiable exogenous shocks | Across categories with non-trivial AI penetration, rank stability is not systematically higher than a null model with no reinforcement would predict |
| **Identity Load** — moderator | Rate of Conditional Reweighting does not vary systematically with measured Identity Load score across categories at comparable AI-mediation availability | Survey, ethnographic, and choice-experiment operationalisations of Identity Load do not show convergent validity |

## 6.2 Evidentiary classes

The paper's claims fall into four classes.

*Class 1 — Empirical generalisation.* Mental Availability, Physical Availability, and the Ehrenberg-Bass empirical generalisations are imported unchanged. The paper makes no new claims in this class.

*Class 2 — Mechanism evidence.* The AIAS Regime 4 evidence supports the claim that AI Availability is empirically distinct from existing consumer-salience constructs (§4). It does not support H1 or H2 directly. It is single-programme evidence under pre-registration, replicated across three categories and one panel-expansion robustness test.

*Class 3 — Framework extension.* H1, H2, and the Identity Load moderator are framework-extension claims with explicit falsification conditions (§6.1). They are not yet directly tested. Each sets a specific empirical agenda (§5.4).

*Class 4 — Predictive-unverified.* The Ehrenberg-Bass generalisations' AI-mediated analogues, the budget-allocation extension from two capitals to three, and category-stratum migration rates are predictive-unverified claims that follow from H1 and H2 holding. None is offered as evidenced; each sets a research-agenda item.

# 7. Conclusion

The paper proposes that AI-mediated retrieval has introduced a third axis of brand presence — AI Availability — operating alongside Mental and Physical Availability rather than displacing them. The extension is conservative in stance: the empirical generalisations of the Ehrenberg-Bass programme are imported unchanged, with their operational definitions preserved and the share-determining role of Mental and Physical Availability unaffected for the consumer-mediated portion of category retrieval. The extension is ambitious in claim: a third system, governed by H1 (Conditional Reweighting) and H2 (Default Reinforcement) and moderated by Identity Load, is necessary to describe brand growth in categories where AI mediation has become material.

The mechanism evidence the paper rests on is pre-registered construct-validity evidence from the AIAS Presence Measurement Programme. Across three independent category studies and a panel-expansion robustness test, AI Presence does not reduce to consumer-search interest at the brand level — the Regime 4 signature supplies the empirical warrant for treating AI Availability as a distinct construct. The evidence supports the warrant for the extension. It does not yet test H1 or H2. The framework states each claim with explicit falsification conditions and assigns each to an evidentiary class accordingly.

The empirical agenda the framework sets — share-explained-variance decomposition for H1, temporal rank-stability measurement for H2, Identity Load operationalisation and convergent-validity testing, and AIAS extension into high-Identity-Load categories (§5.4) — is specific and decomposable. The framework is offered as a starting position for that agenda, not a closure of it. Brand science extended a two-system architecture for thirty years. Whether AI-mediated retrieval now warrants a third is the question the present evidence opens and the framework specifies how to settle.
