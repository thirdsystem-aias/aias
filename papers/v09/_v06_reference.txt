Abstract
This paper reports cross-category measurement of brand visibility in large language model (LLM) outputs
across five consumer categories: project management software, running shoes, premium olive oil,
premium facial skincare, and personal finance applications. A standardized prompt-and-scoring protocol
was applied to two frontier models — Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 and OpenAI gpt-5.4-mini — comprising
six prompts × eight runs per category × two models, producing 96 successful measurements per category
and 480 measurements in total. The measurement establishes baseline rates of brand mention (AI
Presence) per category, per model, and per category-entry-point prompt. Six recurrent patterns are
documented: (1) substantial cross-model variance, with single-brand spreads up to 71 percentage points
and the magnitude of variance tracking discourse fragmentation; (2) systematic asymmetry between
Comparison-frame and Discovery-frame prompts, in which the model converges on incumbents in the
former and elevates a sharply different challenger set in the latter; (3) divergence between LLM mention
rates and external measures of category share, suggesting AI Presence is a distinct construct rather than a
proxy for market visibility; (4) preliminary evidence of discourse-language bias against brands whose
primary marketing discourse is conducted in languages under-represented in AI training corpora; (5)
Default Reinforcement, in which model responses become near-deterministic when training-data
consensus aligns with prompt framing; and (6) phantom-brand surfacing, in which a brand
decommissioned 25 months prior to measurement appeared in 44 percent of model responses about live
products. Construct validity is treated as open and is the proposed object of a Phase 3 designed-for-
confirmation study. The protocol, per-category leaderboards, and underlying datasets are released
alongside this paper.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used by consumers as a recommendation surface in
category decisions, displacing or supplementing search, editorial review, and word of mouth. Which
brands an LLM surfaces in response to a category query, in what relative weights, and across which prompt
frames is a property of the model — its training corpus, its instruction tuning, and the recommendation-
construction logic of its inference layer — and is not directly visible from the brand-tracking instruments
developed for the human-mediated retail era.

This paper reports a cross-category baseline measurement of brand visibility in LLM outputs, hereafter AI
Presence, defined as the rate at which a brand is named in model responses to a standardized set of
category-entry-point prompts. Five consumer categories were measured, selected to span the broadest
available variance on category-structure properties: software and physical goods, B2B and consumer,
mature and emerging, identity-light and identity-heavy, US-centric and globally fragmented production,
low and high regulatory salience.

The research question is descriptive: across categories, models, and prompt frames, what stable patterns
hold in which brands LLMs surface and at what rates? Six patterns are reported. Five replicate across
multiple categories with consistent direction. The sixth — phantom-brand surfacing, in which a brand that
no longer operates appears in 44 percent of model responses about live products in the same category —
emerged from a single category but is the most consequential single observation in the dataset and
motivated a designed-for-test follow-up study (González Castro 2026, v0.7).

Construct validity is treated as open. Whether AI Presence correlates with consumer consideration,
purchase intent, or sales is not established here and is the proposed object of a Phase 3 designed-for-
confirmation study using external brand-tracking data. The findings are reported descriptively as
candidate properties of LLM-mediated brand visibility, not as predictive claims about consumer behavior.

This paper is the empirical baseline companion to the conceptual treatment in González Castro (2026),
"Tri-System Brand Growth: The AI Mediation Layer." The protocol is documented in González Castro
(2026), "AIAS Presence Measurement Protocol v1.1," and the per-category response datasets are released
alongside this preprint.


2. Method

2.1 Categories
Five categories were measured between late March and late April 2026, selected sequentially with each
category chosen to test framework properties not represented by prior measurements. Project
management software was measured first, selected for a mature B2B competitive set with high reported
AI delegation by buyers. Running shoes followed, selected for contrast on physical-versus-software,
consumer-versus-B2B, and substantially higher identity load. Premium olive oil was added third, selected
to introduce a category in which English-language editorial discourse dominates a globally distributed
production base. Premium facial skincare was added fourth, selected to test a category with extremely
coherent editorial consensus dominated by dermatological recommendation logic. Personal finance
applications were added fifth, selected to test a category with regulatory salience, fragmented discourse,
and a recent market disruption (the March 2024 decommissioning of Mint by Intuit).

Collectively, the five categories span identity load (low to very high), buyer type (B2B to consumer),
domain (software, durables, food, beauty, financial services), discourse coherence (very low to very high),
production geography (US-centric to globally fragmented), and regulatory salience (low to high).

2.2 Prompt Protocol
Six prompts were constructed per category, each anchored to a Category Entry Point (CEP) drawn from the
brand-mental-availability literature (Romaniuk and Sharp 2016; Romaniuk 2023): functional-why,
contextual-when, constraint-with, identity-how-feeling, discovery, and comparison. Prompts were
category-specific in their wording but structurally comparable across categories. Each prompt was issued
eight times to each model at temperature 0.7, producing 48 measurements per model per category and 96
measurements per category in total. The full prompt set is documented in the AIAS Presence
Measurement Protocol v1.1.

2.3 Models
Two frontier models were measured: Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 and OpenAI gpt-5.4-mini. Google
Gemini was specified in the original measurement design but excluded from v0.3, v0.4, and v0.6 results
because of a Workspace-domain access restriction during the study window. Cross-model variance
reported here is therefore likely a lower bound on the variance that would be observed with a third
frontier model in the lineup.

2.4 Brand Mention Extraction and Scoring
Brand mentions were extracted via structured-output classification using gpt-5.4-mini at temperature 0,
mapped against a category-specific brand registry, and scored as Presence — the absolute mention rate of
each registered brand across measurements, reported on a 0–100 scale without normalization. Mentions
of brands not in the registry were retained on a category-specific unknown-mentions list and used as a
continuing diagnostic of registry completeness. The full scoring code is released alongside this paper.

2.5 Brand Registry Construction
A category-specific brand registry was constructed prior to first measurement and revised after the first
measurement run if substantial unknown-mention rates indicated registry incompleteness. Two of the
five categories required registry revision: in premium olive oil, the first run surfaced Cobram Estate as the
most-mentioned brand outside the registry, prompting an expansion to include Cobram Estate and three
additional unknown-list brands. In premium facial skincare, the first run surfaced La Roche-Posay,
Vanicream, and six additional dermatologist-recommended drugstore brands as significant unknowns; the
registry was expanded to include all of them. The published per-category data come from post-revision
runs. Earlier runs are preserved in the methodology log for traceability.

Final registry sizes by category: project management software 19 brands, running shoes 17, premium olive
oil 20 (post-revision), premium facial skincare 24 brands surfaced from a registry of approximately 31, and
personal finance applications 16. Registry construction is curated and reflects the framework's view of
each category's competitive set; this represents a methodological choice with direct effects on which
brands appear in measurements, treated as a limitation in §10.

2.6 Total Measurements and Data Release
Each category produced 96 successful measurements; the cross-category total is 480 measurements.
Underlying response datasets are released as presence_index_v0.6_<category>.csv files alongside this
paper. The methodology log is released as methodology.md v0.3, superseded for forward measurements
by AIAS Presence Measurement Protocol v1.1.


3. Three Modes of LLM Response
Across the five categories, model responses to category prompts engaged three structurally distinct
modes, with mode activation depending on the framing of the prompt rather than the structure of the
category. Identifying these modes is foundational to interpreting the patterns reported in §4.

Brand mode is what most of this paper measures. The model is asked about a category and names brands
within that category. CeraVe at 100% in skincare's Identity prompt, YNAB at 100% in personal finance's
Identity prompt, Linear and Asana at 100% in project management software's Comparison prompt — all of
this is brand mode. The model treats the category as a marketplace of named producers and answers
accordingly.

Component mode appeared when the prompt activated a clinical, technical, or specifications frame. The
model shifted from naming brands to naming ingredients, materials, or product properties. In skincare,
the Contextual prompt about fine lines in one's late thirties produced this mode in 95 percent of responses
across both models, with the model naming SPF 30+, retinol, tretinoin, vitamin C, niacinamide, hyaluronic
acid, and peptides rather than brands. Olive oil showed component mode at lower intensity in the
Functional prompt (62 percent), with the model naming "extra virgin olive oil," "EVOO," "light olive oil,"
and "refined olive oil" instead of producer brands.

Authority mode appeared when the prompt asked about discovery, novelty, or where to learn. The model
shifted from naming brands to naming publications, communities, retailers, and competitions. In
skincare's Discovery prompt, the model exclusively named Allure, Byrdie, r/SkincareAddiction, Reddit,
WWD Beauty, Vogue, Sephora, and Ulta. In olive oil's Discovery prompt, the model exclusively named
Olive Oil Times, NYIOOC World Olive Oil Competition, Flos Olei, Bon Appétit, Saveur, and Zingerman's. The
prompt was treated as one of orientation toward a category's information sources, not as one of product
recommendation.

For measurement, this implies that a leaderboard cell with zero brand mentions does not necessarily
indicate a brand's absence from the model's representation of the category. It can indicate that the
prompt activated a different mode, in which the model was producing a different kind of
recommendation. Component mode and authority mode are not measurement failures; they are the
model engaging the question on a different layer of category representation. Future measurement work
will introduce a mode classifier as a pre-step in the measurement pipeline, with brand-mode Presence
reported alongside the rate of component-mode and authority-mode activation. The modes are visible in
the data already collected, and the existing Presence scores remain valid as brand-mode-conditional
measurements.


4. Results
Six recurrent patterns were observed across the five-category dataset. Five replicate across multiple
categories with consistent direction; one (Pattern 6) emerged from a single category. Each pattern is
reported with the underlying category-level evidence and a short interpretive discussion.

4.1 Pattern 1 — Cross-Model Variance and Discourse Coherence
Across all five categories, the same brand can score significantly higher on one frontier model than
another. The variance is consistent enough to be measured, large enough to matter for measurement
validity, and follows a predictable relationship with the structure of the underlying category discourse.
The largest single-brand variance observed across the five-category dataset comes from personal finance.
Rocket Money was mentioned in 71 percent of OpenAI responses about personal finance applications and
zero percent of Anthropic responses — two models, asked the same six prompts about the same category,
producing effectively disjoint answers about whether Rocket Money exists as a category player. Mint, the
decommissioned Intuit application, showed a 42-point spread in the same direction (Anthropic 65
percent, OpenAI 23 percent). Five other personal finance brands had spreads above 14 points.

Premium olive oil produced spreads nearly as wide. Frescobaldi Laudemio appeared in 42 percent of
Anthropic responses and 2 percent of OpenAI responses (40-point spread). Castillo de Canena showed the
inverse asymmetry. Frantoio Muraglia appeared in 15 percent of Anthropic responses and 42 percent of
OpenAI responses. Project management software showed similar magnitudes for top brands: ClickUp
surfaced in 83 percent of OpenAI responses and 42 percent of Anthropic responses (41-point spread).
Asana showed inverse asymmetry, dominant on OpenAI at 94 percent and softer on Anthropic at 67
percent.

Running shoes and skincare produced markedly narrower variance. The largest top-brand spread in
running shoes was Hoka at 19 points. In skincare, CeraVe registered identical 67 percent Presence on both
models — zero spread, the only zero-spread leader observed across any category. The largest top-brand
spread in skincare was Paula's Choice at 23 points.




The pattern across five categories points to a structural relationship. Variance is not a function of category
maturity (the v0.4 hypothesis): olive oil is mature and produces high variance, while running shoes is
similarly mature and produces low variance. The unifying variable is discourse coherence. Personal finance
has the most fragmented discourse in the dataset — split across YouTube creators, traditional financial
media, fintech press, Reddit communities, and influencer recommendations, none of which agree about
which brands deserve consideration. Olive oil discourse fragments across geographies, production
traditions, and audience tiers. Project management software discourse fragments across competing
professional frames. Running shoes discourse converges around a small number of authoritative voices
speaking in one language about a small set of technical criteria. Skincare discourse converges even more
tightly around dermatological recommendation logic. The two converged-discourse categories produce
narrow variance; the three fragmented-discourse categories produce wide variance.

For measurement validity, this implies that single-model AI-visibility audits sample a slice of a larger and
more variable surface in fragmented-discourse categories. Multi-model measurement is required for
cross-category claims about LLM brand visibility to be substantively defensible.

4.2 Pattern 2 — Comparison vs. Discovery Asymmetry
Across all five categories, models converged on incumbent brands when prompts asked them to compare
options, and elevated a sharply different brand set when prompts asked about emerging or novel options.
The asymmetry is consistent across categories. The strength varies.

In premium olive oil, the comparison prompt returned California Olive Ranch and Cobram Estate at near-
saturation; the discovery prompt elevated Brightland, Graza, and Kosterina, three direct-to-consumer
brands whose combined sales are a fraction of the comparison-prompt leaders. In running shoes, the
comparison prompt returned Nike, Asics, and New Balance at 100 percent; the discovery prompt returned
Norda, a four-year-old Canadian trail-running brand, at 100 percent. In project management software,
comparison returned Asana, Monday, and Jira at 100 percent; discovery surfaced Linear and Height. In
personal finance, comparison returned YNAB, Empower, and Monarch Money; discovery (which named
Mint by name in the prompt) elevated Monarch and Copilot.

Skincare is the structural exception. The comparison prompt produced strong convergence on CeraVe,
Drunk Elephant, and Skinceuticals. But the discovery prompt did not produce a clear "emerging" tier —
Tatcha, Augustinus Bader, and Youth to the People surfaced at 25 percent, 25 percent, and 12 percent
respectively, with no brand achieving the 50 percent threshold seen in other categories. The asymmetry
exists in skincare; it is weaker. The mechanism appears to be that skincare's editorial discourse has been
sufficiently worked through that no brand confidently occupies an "emerging" cognitive slot in the model's
recommendation logic.
Figure 2. Comparison prompts converge on incumbents; Discovery prompts elevate a sharply different set. The
asymmetry repeats across all five categories — sharpest in PM software, running shoes, and olive oil.

Three sub-findings are strengthened by five-category replication. First, the model's working definition of
"emerging" is unstable across categories and consistently does not mean "new." In project management
software, the model's emerging-tools list included Notion, valued in the tens of billions of dollars. In olive
oil, discovery-prompt leaders Brightland and Kosterina are seven and eight years old respectively. In
personal finance, the discovery prompt named YNAB at 100 percent — a 17-year-old company. The
model's working definition of "emerging" appears to mean "non-dominant in the comparison frame," not
"young," "small," or "novel."

Second, challenger brands can achieve concentrated AI Presence in narrow CEPs without registering on
broader prompts. Norda's overall Presence in running shoes is 17 percent; its Discovery Presence is 100
percent. Kosterina's overall Presence in olive oil is 27 percent; its Discovery Presence is 60 percent.
Augustinus Bader's overall Presence in skincare is 7 percent; its Discovery Presence is 25 percent.
Concentrated CEP visibility is not visible from category-level Presence.
Third, prompt design materially affects which version of the asymmetry appears. Discovery prompts that
name a specific market disruption (the personal finance prompt naming Mint) produce strong
convergence. Discovery prompts that ask generically about "emerging brands" produce diffuse responses,
particularly in categories with no clearly defined emerging tier. Measurement programs that use generic
discovery prompts will under-detect the visibility available in narrower CEP framings.

4.3 Pattern 3 — Divergence from Consumer Awareness
Across all five categories, AI Presence does not track external measures of consumer awareness, and the
gap takes different forms depending on the category's structure. In four of the five categories, divergence
runs one direction: brands with high consumer awareness underperform their market position in AI
mediation. In personal finance, the divergence inverts: a brand with modest market share dominates AI
mediation completely.

The four downward-divergence cases form a clear pattern. Premium olive oil shows it in extreme form: the
four most distributed legacy supermarket brands — Bertolli, Colavita, Goya, and Filippo Berio — combine
for less than 8 percent AI Presence in aggregate. Goya, despite being one of the largest Latin foods brands
in the United States, scored exactly zero. Brightland, a seven-year-old direct-to-consumer brand that
exists in only a small fraction of those brands' retail footprint, scored 46 percent. Skincare shows the
divergence in even sharper form: La Mer, SK-II, Lancôme, Estée Lauder, Clinique, and Olay — six legacy
luxury heritage brands — combine to roughly 6 percent Presence. CeraVe, the dermatologist-
recommended drugstore brand, scored 67 percent alone. Running shoes shows the divergence in less
extreme but still strong form: Nike has the largest marketing budget of any brand in the running-shoe
category and arguably the highest cultural saturation of any brand globally; in AI Presence Nike places
sixth at 57 percent, behind Asics, Brooks, Saucony, New Balance, and Hoka. Brooks, with a marketing
budget that is a fraction of Nike's, ties for first at 80 percent. Project management software shows the
divergence in mild form, with AI Presence and traditional consideration tracking more closely at the top of
the leaderboard.
The fifth case — personal finance — inverts the direction. YNAB, with approximately one million paid
subscribers, scored 100 percent AI Presence across every prompt and every model. Rocket Money, with
five times more users, surfaced in zero percent of Anthropic responses and 71 percent of OpenAI
responses. EveryDollar, the budgeting application from Ramsey Solutions with millions of users in its
ecosystem, scored 25 percent. Empower, the platform for higher net-worth users, scored 66 percent. The
brand with the smallest user base in the top tier dominates the category in AI mediation; brands with
substantially larger user bases trail or splinter across models.

The mechanism connecting the five cases is consistent. AI training corpora accumulate editorial framings
over time. Where editorial discourse builds a coherent quality narrative aligned with a specific brand's
positioning, that brand's AI Presence outpaces its commercial scale. CeraVe is the most consistently
recommended brand in dermatological discourse, not the largest skincare brand by revenue. YNAB is the
most methodologically distinctive brand in financial-discipline discourse, not the largest personal finance
brand by users. The AI tier appears to privilege discourse position over distribution scale, and the
asymmetry runs in both directions: upward when small brands have strong narratives, downward when
large brands lack them.

The construct-validity implication is direct. AI Presence does not function as a proxy for awareness or for
distribution scale. Whether it functions as a leading indicator of consumer behavior — predicting shifts in
consideration or share — or as a measurement of LLM behavior in isolation cannot be determined from
this dataset and is the proposed object of a Phase 3 designed-for-confirmation study against external
brand-tracking data.
4.4 Pattern 4 — Discourse-Language Bias (Preliminary)
The fourth pattern emerged in two of the five categories with consistent direction across different
national-origin contexts. It is reported as preliminary because no measurement to date was designed
specifically to test the hypothesis. Two categories produce a consistent signal; a third designed-for-test
category will be required to determine its status.

In premium olive oil, three brands were added to the registry specifically to ensure Spanish
representation. Spain produces approximately 45 percent of global olive oil. Goya is one of the most
widely distributed Spanish-affiliated brands in the United States. Castillo de Canena is a premium
Andalusian estate brand with growing US specialty distribution. Núñez de Prado is a respected family-
owned Andalusian producer with strong editorial presence in Spanish-language food media. Their AI
Presence scores were 0 percent, 23 percent, and 5 percent respectively. None placed in the top five. The
category leaders were California Olive Ranch (US), Cobram Estate (Australia), Brightland (US), Frantoio
Muraglia (Italy), and Graza (US). Italian brands collectively performed in line with their US commercial
profile. American and Australian brands over-performed relative to global production share. Spanish
brands under-performed substantially.

In premium facial skincare, Beauty of Joseon was included in the registry as a representative of K-beauty's
strongest current brand presence in US specialty retail. Korean cosmetics enjoy significant English-
language coverage in major beauty media — Allure, Vogue, Glamour, and The Strategist have published
extensively on K-beauty for over a decade. Beauty of Joseon scored zero across both runs of the skincare
measurement. The skincare unknowns list, which surfaced eight additional missing brands, contained no
non-English-discourse brands at all. Every brand the model surfaced as a category player was an American
direct-to-consumer brand or a French/American clinical brand.
Figure 4. Country-of-origin two-panel: olive oil (left) and premium skincare (right). Spanish brands under-perform in
olive oil; Korean brands are absent from skincare — in both cases, English-language discourse dominates the AI
training corpus.

The most parsimonious explanation is that AI training corpora over-represent English-language discourse
on these categories, and the bias persists even when the cultural reference of the brand is well-covered in
English. American food magazines, Italian food media translated into English, and US-based recipe
websites dominate the digital corpus on olive oil. American beauty magazines and US-based dermatology
blogs dominate the corpus on skincare. Spanish-language food media and Korean-language beauty media,
even when authoritative within their domestic markets, do not propagate into English-language AI
training data at the rates that comparable English-language sources do.

A subtle refinement of the hypothesis becomes visible across the two cases. Tatcha, a Japanese-themed
skincare brand founded in San Francisco with American-English marketing, scored 18 percent Presence in
skincare. Beauty of Joseon, a Korean brand with Korean-language primary marketing despite some US-
targeted English content, scored zero. The cultural reference (Japanese aesthetic) does not appear to be
the constraint. The marketing-discourse language does. The refined hypothesis: LLMs under-surface
brands whose primary marketing discourse is conducted in a language under-represented in AI training
data, even when the cultural reference is well-known in English.

Authority-mode responses across the two affected categories provide additional, unexpected evidence.
When the model was asked about discovery in skincare and olive oil, every publication, community, and
retailer it named was English-language: Allure, Byrdie, Vogue, and WWD Beauty in skincare; Olive Oil
Times, Flos Olei, Bon Appétit, and Saveur in olive oil. Spanish-language food media, Korean-language
beauty media, Japanese, Italian, French, and German publications respected within their domestic
categories did not appear. The discourse-language bias in brand recommendations is consistent with a
deeper bias in which media systems are recognized as authoritative.

The pattern remains preliminary in three respects: it is based on two categories with single nationally-
skewed signals each; it involves small absolute numbers of test brands; and alternative explanations — US
retail availability, marketing investment in English-targeted channels, or category-specific distribution
structures — have not been controlled for. A more rigorous test requires a category in which a non-
English-discourse country dominates global production but has limited US-targeted English-language
marketing. Japanese kitchen knives, French wine, and Korean small electronics are candidate categories
for a designed-for-test follow-up.

4.5 Pattern 5 — Default Reinforcement and Discourse-Prompt Alignment
Pattern 5 is closely connected to Pattern 1 (discourse coherence) and Pattern 3 (divergence from
awareness) but distinct from both. It describes how the strength of incumbent dominance in AI mediation
depends not just on the underlying discourse coherence, but on how well-aligned that discourse is with
the framing of the prompt itself.

Skincare provides the cleanest illustration. The Identity prompt asked: "What skincare brands do
dermatologists actually recommend to their patients?" CeraVe, Cetaphil, and Neutrogena each appeared
at 100 percent mention rate — three brands tied at perfect saturation across 96 measurements. The
Constraint prompt about sensitive skin returned CeraVe, La Roche-Posay, and Vanicream each at 100
percent. The Functional prompt about moisturizers for normal-to-dry skin returned CeraVe at 100
percent. Across three prompts framed in three different ways, responses converged on the same small set
of dermatologist-recommended drugstore brands.

The mechanism is observable: skincare's editorial discourse has built a tight, well-documented consensus
that dermatologists recommend specific clinical-positioning brands, and the prompts in this study
repeatedly aligned with that consensus. When the model's training data carries strong consensus and the
prompt's cognitive frame activates that consensus, the response is nearly deterministic — the same
brands appear at saturation across measurements.
Personal finance shows a parallel pattern with different content. The Identity prompt asked about apps
that "people who are actually good with money use," loaded but ambiguous identity language. YNAB and
Empower both scored 100 percent. The Comparison prompt produced YNAB, Empower, and Monarch
Money each at 100 percent. The Constraint prompt about partners managing money together produced
YNAB and Monarch Money each at 100 percent. Across multiple identity frames, responses converged on
the same small brand set with deterministic confidence.

Running shoes and project management software show the contrast. In running shoes, the Identity
prompt ("what serious competitive runners actually wear") produced Nike at 100 percent — but no other
brand reached 100 percent on that prompt. The category's discourse is more contested between cultural-
narrative and technical-recommendation framings; both surface, depending on which CEP is activated. In
project management software, the Identity prompt produced multiple brands across both models without
any single brand reaching saturation. The category's discourse fragments across multiple identity-loaded
frames (developer-first, marketing-first, generalist), none of which dominates.

The cross-category data suggest a structural relationship: the strength of Default Reinforcement at the
prompt level is a function of how completely the category's editorial consensus aligns with the prompt's
framing. When alignment is high (skincare's dermatologist prompts, personal finance's discipline
prompts), responses are nearly deterministic. When alignment is partial (running shoes' identity prompt,
PM software's frame-divided prompts), responses are stratified. When alignment is low, responses are
diffuse.

4.6 Pattern 6 — Phantom-Brand Surfacing (Single-Category Observation)
Pattern 6 emerged from a single category measurement but is sufficiently striking to warrant inclusion as a
standalone observation. It describes a structural property of LLM-mediated brand visibility that no prior
pattern captures: the temporal lag between actual market reality and the model's representation of the
category.
In March 2024, Intuit decommissioned Mint, its consumer personal finance application that had been the
dominant brand in the category for over a decade. Existing users were migrated to Credit Karma; the Mint
application was retired; the brand effectively ceased to exist as a current consumer product. By April 2026,
when this measurement was conducted, Mint had not been a live application for 25 months.

In the personal finance measurement reported here, Mint was mentioned in 44 percent of model
responses about personal finance applications. Among brands with non-zero Presence scores, Mint placed
fifth — ahead of Rocket Money, EveryDollar, Quicken Simplifi, and PocketGuard, all of which are live
applications with active user bases. The model surfaced a brand that had not existed for over two years
more frequently than it surfaced multiple applications consumers can currently use.




Per-model breakdown reveals an additional dimension. On Anthropic, Mint surfaced in 65 percent of
responses; on OpenAI, in 23 percent. The 42-point spread is the second-largest in the category dataset and
suggests meaningfully different training-data freshness across the two models on this specific category
change. Both systems lag actual market reality, in different ways and to different extents.

The candidate mechanism is straightforward. AI training corpora are necessarily backward-looking. They
capture the discourse that existed when the data was collected, not the discourse that exists at the
moment a query is made. For a brand that had been dominant for over a decade and had accumulated
proportionate volume of editorial coverage, blog posts, comparison articles, and how-to guides, that
backward-facing volume continues to surface in responses long after the brand itself has stopped existing.
The model has no mechanism to remove a brand from its training corpus when the brand shuts down. The
corpus simply ages, and the brand persists within it.

A single-category basis limits this finding's current generalizability. Other category disruptions — the
Twitter rebrand to X, GE's corporate splits, the Toys R Us shutdown and revival, the Bed Bath & Beyond
bankruptcy and online-only revival, several pharmaceutical brand transitions — would each provide
additional test cases. A designed-for-test follow-up was conducted for the Bed Bath & Beyond case, with
eight pre-registered hypotheses including a structural comparator (Pier 1) and a revised theoretical
framing of the phantom as a recommendation-slot persistence rather than a knowledge-freshness lag
(González Castro 2026, v0.7).

Treated as a candidate property of LLM-mediated brand visibility, the phantom finding implies that AI
Presence is a hybrid signal: partly reflective of current market position, partly reflective of accumulated
editorial weight in the training corpus extending backward several years, and partly reflective of category
disruptions that have not propagated through the model's training pipeline. The construct-validity
implication of this hybridity is that AI Presence cannot be interpreted as a snapshot of present market
reality without further calibration against external behavioral data.

6. Discussion: Construct Validity
The patterns reported in §4 establish AI Presence as a measurable property of the LLM tier with stable
structure across categories, models, and prompt frames. They do not establish whether AI Presence
correlates with consumer consideration, purchase intent, or sales — that is a separate empirical question.

The downward-divergence cases in Pattern 3 (luxury heritage skincare brands at low Presence; legacy
supermarket olive oil brands at near-zero Presence) and the upward-divergence case (YNAB at 100
percent on a small subscriber base) are inconsistent with the hypothesis that AI Presence is a proxy for
awareness or distribution scale. The phantom finding in Pattern 6 (Mint at 44 percent 25 months after
decommissioning) is inconsistent with the hypothesis that AI Presence is a snapshot of current market
reality. These two observations together suggest that AI Presence is a distinct construct that requires its
own validation rather than reduction to pre-existing brand-tracking metrics.
A construct-validity test against external brand-tracking data is therefore not optional but
methodologically required before AI Presence can be interpreted as a leading indicator of consumer
behavior. A weak correlation would establish the index as a measurement of LLM behavior alone, with
implications limited to channel optimization for AI-mediated visibility. A strong correlation would establish
AI Presence as a leading indicator with predictive value beyond the LLM tier. Both outcomes are
publishable. Neither has been established here.


7. Limitations
Six limitations apply to the findings reported here.

Sample size. Each category measurement comprises 96 successful API calls, producing approximately ten-
percentage-point confidence bands around individual brand Presence scores. The cross-category patterns
reported survive that uncertainty because the patterns are large relative to the band. Smaller margins
between specific brands within a category should not be treated as decisive.

Two models, not three. The original measurement design included Google Gemini, which was excluded
from v0.3, v0.4, and v0.6 results because of a Workspace-domain access restriction during the study
window. Cross-model variance reported here is likely a lower bound; introducing a third frontier model
historically widens the range, which would strengthen the variance-related findings.

Five categories, not many. Five categories is sufficient to claim replication of patterns across diverse
contexts. It is not sufficient to claim that the patterns hold in general. Continued cross-category
measurement, including categories selected explicitly to test framework predictions (e.g., the discourse-
language bias hypothesis in Pattern 4), is required before universal claims become defensible.

A single point in time per category. Each category was measured on a single day in late April 2026. The
findings describe model behavior on those days. They do not describe how AI brand visibility moves over
time. Frontier models are updated frequently, often without external notice. Longitudinal measurement
requires periodic re-baselining. The phantom-brand surfacing pattern (Pattern 6) is the most time-
sensitive of the findings; its strength may evolve as training corpora refresh.

Construct validity unproven. As discussed in §6, AI Presence measures a real and stable property of the
LLM tier, but whether that property correlates with consumer consideration, purchase intent, or sales is
unknown. AI Presence should be treated as a measurement of LLM behavior with unverified predictive
value.

Registry construction is not perfectly neutral. The brand registry for each category is curated and reflects
the framework's view of which brands constitute the category's competitive set. Two of the five categories
required registry revision after the first measurement. The unknown-mentions list provides a continuing
diagnostic, but registry construction remains the methodological choice with the most direct effect on
which brands appear in measurements.
8. Future Research
Three priorities follow from the findings reported here. First, a designed-for-test category to test the
discourse-language bias hypothesis (Pattern 4): a category in which a non-English-discourse country
dominates global production but has limited US-targeted English-language marketing (candidate
categories: Japanese kitchen knives, French wine, Korean small electronics). Second, a designed-for-test
category to test phantom-brand surfacing (Pattern 6) in a different category context. The Bed Bath &
Beyond bankruptcy and online-only revival provided such a test and is reported as González Castro (2026,
v0.7); additional cases (the Twitter-to-X transition, several pharmaceutical brand transitions) would
extend coverage. Third, a Phase 3 construct-validity study correlating AI Presence scores against
consumer-tracking data from the same windows.
